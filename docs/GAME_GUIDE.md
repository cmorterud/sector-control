# Sector Control game guide

Detailed reference for mechanics, maps and development scenarios, accumulated as features were added; newer notes within a section supersede older ones. Start with the [README](../README.md). [HANDOFF.md](HANDOFF.md) holds the development log: project decisions, implementation state, validation and recorded-playtest status.

Local single-player shooter with toggleable first- and third-person views. Ypres is 50v50 domination with four flags; Village front and Verdun sector have five flags. Matches last up to eight minutes.

Try [Verdun sector](https://sector-control-trial-20261108.cody22597.chatgpt.site/?map=verdun), [Village front](https://sector-control-trial-20261108.cody22597.chatgpt.site/?map=village) or [Ypres](https://sector-control-trial-20261108.cody22597.chatgpt.site/?map=trenches). Anyone with the link can play without signing in. Use a desktop browser with a keyboard and mouse; click **Begin operation** to start. [Start trenches in first person](https://sector-control-trial-20261108.cody22597.chatgpt.site/?map=trenches&view=first), or press T during play. Each visitor plays an independent match against bots.

This is a frozen production-build snapshot, updated November 14, 2026 through Sites. Later game commits do not update the trial automatically; refresh the separate hosting snapshot and deploy it explicitly. The URL has no configured automatic expiration. Deployment identity and verification details are in HANDOFF.md.

Trench and village terrain now use close-up clumped earth, grit and broken soil fissures alongside broad wet/dry/grass variation. Explosions replace excavated grass, paving and wet surface finishes with fresh earth; the detail follows steep banks and crater walls without stretching. Individual worn duckboard planks, split timber grain, small exposed roots and rounded rubble add trench detail. Terrain lighting now shares smooth normals across patch seams, decodes surface colors consistently and uses softer soil highlights to prevent bright bands on trench banks. These are cosmetic changes; movement and cover collision keep their existing shapes.

Artillery destruction reuses unchanged terrain cells and existing mesh buffers. Visual uploads, ragdoll setup, bot navigation rebuilds and reshaped terrain patches are spread over time, while damage and the simulation's own terrain heights update immediately. In the browser, terrain patches are rebuilt nearest the camera first, at least one per frame within a 4 ms budget, so bullets and sight lines can meet the old ground near a fresh crater for a frame or two. Bots retain unaffected routes and check the next segment against current terrain. In the browser, navigation rebuilds get 3 ms of each frame, shared by every tick in it, and at most two simulation ticks run per frame, so one slow frame after a blast does not cause a burst of catch-up work in the next. Settled corpses keep their visible pose outside the physics solver and reactivate for nearby blasts or supporting-ground/cover loss. Corpse meshes are shared per kit and prepared during deployment. Validation results and current browser-performance verification limits are documented in HANDOFF.md.

## Player options and equipment

**Controls** in the briefing and pause menu remaps actions, switches hold/toggle sprint (hold Shift by default), and saves camera and keyboard preferences locally. Escape always pauses/resumes. Reset restores defaults. Browser modifier shortcuts and duplicate bindings are rejected; browser-owned shortcuts can still close a tab.

Choose **Rifleman**, **Marksman** or **Support** before deployment. Changes during a match apply on respawn. Press **1** for the primary and **2** for the pistol, or use your remapped keys. Trying to fire an empty weapon starts its normal reload when reserve ammo remains; R still reloads manually. Ammo and bolt cooldown belong to each weapon; switching cancels a reload without adding ammo. Support weapons fire automatically while held and become less accurate while moving. Marksmen have a fixed 4× scope, half-second sight settling and reduced peripheral vision. Pistols deal 25 damage and have a 25 m effective range.

Western Front maps use British and Imperial German uniforms and equipment; **Verdun sector** uses France and Imperial Germany. The procedural models are stylized faction variants with shared role statistics: SMLE / Gewehr 98 / Berthier rifles, Lewis / MG 08/15 / Chauchat support weapons, and Webley / P08 / Ruby sidearms. French marksmen carry a scoped Lebel-inspired rifle. These are gameplay archetypes, not exact museum replicas. Their new models have separate furniture, sights and actions: full-stock SMLE, long-barrel Gewehr, slender Berthier and tubular Lebel; Lewis cooling shroud/pan, MG water jacket/belt and Chauchat crescent magazine; opening-frame Webley, toggle-action P08 and compact Ruby slide.

The first model upgrade covers French/German soldiers and the Berthier/Gewehr rifles: shaped coats, sleeves, faces, helmets and boots; cloth/leather/metal surface detail; curved stocks and darker machined steel; grip hands with fingers/thumbs; and extra first-person weapon details. The close view has independent detailed buffers. Distant French/German torso/head/equipment meshes simplify beyond42m and return within32m; the human stays detailed. Limb/weapon batches remain, while the torso mesh changes independently. Upgraded surfaces carry into ragdolls. Reload/bolt timings, sights, projectiles and hit volumes are unchanged. Browser/GPU performance acceptance remains unverified; see HANDOFF.md.

Nearby soldiers now plant their boots against the terrain with stance anchors and leg IK, adapting to slopes and small steps while keeping the original leg lengths. Crouch/stand, acceleration/braking, turns and mantle entry blend more naturally. Curved reload hand paths, palm-attached carried ammunition, bolt-following wrists and eased shoulder contact improve handling. These are presentation changes: movement, collision, hit volumes, camera/sight rays and reload/bolt timing remain unchanged. Distant bots retain a cheaper gait. See HANDOFF.md for the completed checks and interrupted native performance review.

Two rare **flame kits** are placed in forward trenches on each historical map. Press **E** nearby to equip the kit and keep your pistol. A kit has eight seconds of fuel, an 8 m jet, 30 damage per second and a brief nonstacking burn. Earth, cover and tank armor stop the jet, and wooden cover can burn. Enemy bots react to visible flame and seek cover. When the kit runs dry, press **E** to drop it and regain access to your original primary. Death drops any remaining fuel. Supply replenishes two minutes after a drop, with up to two kits in circulation. Only players pick up kits.

Ypres, Industrial and Verdun have game screenshots in their selection cards. Village uses a labeled layout illustration matching its new nine-building arrangement; a new gameplay screenshot awaits rendered review. The tactical map draws terrain and current cover, updating after destruction. Verdun is a 208 × 128 m, five-flag battlefield set at Fleury and Thiaumont on 23 June 1916, when the Germans attacked toward Fort Souville behind the first big phosgene barrage. It has woodland, crater and communication-trench routes. The flags take the names of real positions in the sector: A Fleury and B Chapitre Wood toward the French side, D Dame Ravine and E Hardaumont Work toward the German side. Its central C flag, Thiaumont, is a shallow shell-torn clearing with four dogleg approaches, a broken signal mast, severed wires, a collapsed dugout and destructible low cover. It is an authored layout rather than a geographical reconstruction: the names are real, their positions on the map are approximate.

Artillery cooldowns, fixed objectives and blast rules remain unchanged. Snapshots and playtest summaries now include repeat calls near flags, blast deaths, capture interruptions and potential damage prevented by cover. `tools/battle-audit.ts` runs three seeded full matches to gather evidence before proposing balance changes. See HANDOFF.md for results and validation limits.

## Run

Requires Node.js 22.12+ (developed with Node 26).

```sh
npm ci
npm run dev
```

Open the printed localhost URL, then click **Begin operation** to capture the mouse. WASD moves, mouse aims, Shift sprints, T switches first/third person, holding right mouse aims through the iron sights, each left click fires one round, R reloads, Tab holds the scoreboard, and Escape pauses. Press Escape again to resume, or click Resume. After Escape releases the mouse, the next movement/action key or a click on the game recaptures it automatically; that capture click does not fire a shot. Losing focus pauses the local simulation.

```sh
npm run check
npm run build
npm run preview
```

### Spawning along the front

On Ypres (by day and at night), Verdun sector, Spring Offensive, Village front and Lighthouse point each side deploys in a line along the whole length of its front, at its spawn's distance from the enemy, instead of in one group. At the start of a match the soldiers stand roughly 2.5 m apart from one flank to the other, and each soldier respawns at their own place in that line, so respawns come in all along it too. You start in the middle; your squadmates stand beside you. No place is on a flag (within 9 m of one), in a wall, the wire, the sea or beside a tank. Stray heavy shells keep 20 m off the line and gas shells 24 m. Fort Vaux, the Zeppelin, Training and the Industrial district keep their own spawns.

Use **Redeploy** in the pause menu to die at your current position and return to your team spawn after the normal five-second delay. It adds one death without awarding a kill or changing domination team scores. In a domination match's final phase it spends a reinforcement like any other respawn, and it is unavailable once your team has none left.

Use **Select map** in the pause menu to leave the current match and open another battlefield’s start screen. The results screen also offers map choices. Your selected camera view carries over.

GitHub Actions runs `npm ci`, `npm run check -- --maxWorkers=1` (TypeScript and tests), and `npm run build` on every push to `main`. The [Build and test workflow](../.github/workflows/ci.yml) uses Node.js 26 on Ubuntu with npm caching. It can also be started manually from the repository's Actions tab.

Development scenarios: `?bots=0` disables bots; `?duration=30` runs a thirty-second match; `?demo=1` permits observation without pointer lock; `?overview=1&autoplay=1` gives a top-down observer view for reviewing the layout and bot routes. These do not change normal gameplay defaults.

Press **T** to toggle the normal view. First person is the default for a fresh profile; the camera choice is saved locally. `?view=first` or `?view=third` overrides it for that visit. Hold **right mouse** for first-person iron sights from either view, with a narrower field of view and slower mouse sensitivity. Releasing returns to the chosen view. The camera sits at the standing/crouched eye; the local head/body are hidden and separate articulated arms retain the reload/bolt/throw motions. Sights align with the camera ray, and traveling rounds converge from the barrel onto the first surface or soldier under the sight. Nearby cover can still block the barrel. Recoil moves the view and shot aim together; cosmetic blast rotation does not displace first-person sights. Grenade throws keep the selected perspective. In first person, the rifle pulls inward near solid cover (including its bayonet), lowers during actual sprint movement, and smoothly shoulders again when stopping or aiming. Retraction does not let shots bypass cover. Settled sights retain their alignment with the camera ray.

Walk over a trench edge to drop in naturally; no Space press is needed. Gravity carries you onto the firing step or floor, with a small landing pose. Press **Space** to hop on open ground or mantle upward to climb out. Hopping over an edge continues the arc down to the ground below.

Trench riflemen, including the player, carry a **bolt-action service rifle** with contoured walnut furniture, rounded steel, faction-specific sights, magazine and bolt geometry. Each uses shared detailed meshes and procedural wood/steel finishes in both views. It carries five rounds and 60 reserve. Click once to fire; the right hand opens, pulls and closes the faction bolt before another shot is possible (0.6 seconds between shots). Press R for a 1.6-second reload. SMLE, Gewehr and Berthier load a clip; the Lebel pose feeds loose cartridges. Service rounds deal 50 damage and have stronger recoil, a deeper report and timed mechanical sounds. Industrial keeps its 30-round semi-auto rifle. Holding the mouse does not repeat fire; a press during reload or cycling must be released before trying again.

Inside **Fort Vaux**, French and German riflemen carry a **carbine** instead: the Berthier Mle 1892 M16 and the Karabiner 98AZ. Each is its rifle with 18 cm off the barrel, the same five rounds and the same hit up close, worked faster (a shot every 0.87 s against 1.1 s). Past 25 m its damage falls away, to 70% at 60 m. Marksmen, machine gunners and sidearms are unchanged.

Weapon changes lower the old gun, draw the selected one and raise it over the existing **0.3-second** firing lock. Ordinary sights shoulder over **0.2 seconds**; marksmen retain their **0.5-second** settling. The visible first-person muzzle and firing origin share that shoulder curve. Reload hands follow the actual feed location for pans, belts, curved magazines, pistol magazines and opening revolver frames. Slides/toggles and bolt travel follow the weapon; magazine/bolt/equip sounds follow their animation phases. Reloading during a draw queues a full loading cycle. Shared role capacities, damage, fire rates and reload durations remain unchanged, including the common eight-round sidearm capacity despite the Webley's stylized six-chamber model.

Service-rifle, semi-auto rifle and pistol rounds travel at **192 m/s**; machine-gun rounds travel at **48 m/s**. All bullet speeds are 1.5× the previous values. All retain a 60 m range, visible tracers, swept collision and damage on impact; bot lead follows each weapon’s speed.

Reloads now take half their previous time: **1 second** for the semi-auto rifle, **1.6 seconds** for the service rifle and **1.5 seconds** for the machine gun. Hand, clip/magazine and bolt animations follow the shorter timings, with mechanical sound cues aligned to them.

**Machine gunners:** four bots per team in 50v50 (two in 25v25) carry a longer wooden-stock weapon with a pan magazine and folded bipod. They fire four-to-six-round bursts at 600 rounds/minute, then pause for 1.5–3 seconds. They retain recognition delay, imperfect aim, recoil, suppression and sight-loss interruption, with more aim wander than riflemen. Their magazines hold 60 rounds with 240 reserve and a 1.5-second reload. They stay with the covering force rather than joining assault squads. Players can choose Rifleman, Marksman or Support before deployment or for their next respawn. Small 5v5 bot rosters stay rifle-only.

Rifles now kick upward and slightly sideways on every shot. The kick moves the barrel, shoulder and camera and changes subsequent projectile directions for players and bots. Pull down to compensate, or space your shots and let the rifle settle. Right-mouse aiming reduces recoil by 20%; crouching reduces it by another 15%. The support hand stays on the grip.

Soldiers visibly brace and twist their shoulders after close enemy bullet passes, hits and nearby landed explosions. Bots briefly crouch and pause their shooting, then recover; a bounded local search can move an exposed bot a few meters toward reachable cover against the incoming direction. Destroyed shelter and new fire from another direction trigger a recheck. Reaction cooldowns leave time to return fire and keep pursuing flags. These cues do not reveal the shooter's identity or position to the targeting system. Human movement, stance and aim stay under player control, and first-person sights retain their alignment. Playtest logs include `combatReaction` and `reactionCover` events plus cover-arrival counters.

Press **V** for melee. Trench service rifles use a **bayonet thrust**: 65 damage, a short wind-up, 1.8 m reach and recovery between attacks. Build up forward sprint speed, then press V for a **bayonet charge**: 100 damage and limited turning. Keep sprinting forward to hold the bayonet raised and readied until the first body contact, which lowers it and starts a longer recovery. Releasing sprint or forward movement, hitting solid cover, becoming airborne, or dying ends the charge. Allies and protected soldiers can absorb the first contact without damage. Machine guns and industrial rifles use a shorter, weaker weapon-butt strike (45 damage, 1.25 m reach). Holding V does not repeat attacks.

An active charge visibly raises and slightly centers the rifle, with gentle side-to-side and fore/aft movement tied to the running stride in both views. Both hands stay on the weapon; the extra charge motion clears on contact or cancellation.

Melee checks the victim's position at contact, so stepping away can make it miss. Earth and solid cover stop it, and friendly/protected soldiers block the thrust without taking damage. An attack interrupts firing, loading, throwing and climbing until recovery ends. You cannot begin while reloading, throwing, airborne or stunned. A landed blow briefly staggers a surviving soldier and can interrupt their wind-up. Bots thrust only against a nearby recognized enemy, after a close-range reaction delay; artillery evasion and suppression cover still take priority. Bots also charge (below).

### Bayonet charges and going over the top

While you sprint forward with a bayoneted service rifle and could charge, the HUD shows **V · BAYONET CHARGE** (with your melee key) under the reticle; during the charge it reads **BAYONET CHARGE · KEEP SPRINTING**. The prompt uses the same test as the charge itself: service rifle, sprint held, moving forward at 5.5 m/s or more, not aiming, crouched, reloading, throwing, airborne, staggered or recovering. The key strip lists the charge and the whistle.

Press **Z** (remappable as *Whistle: squad bayonet charge*) to blow your whistle. Up to ten friendly bot riflemen follow: the squad of the nearest friendly bot within 30 m, then any other rifleman within 18 m. Machine gunners and mounted guns keep covering. They pause a moment to fix bayonets, then sprint in a line abreast, at least 2.2 m apart, toward where you aim: an enemy or neutral flag within about 23° of your aim and 70 m, otherwise the enemy front trench in that direction, otherwise the ground you aim at (12–45 m). Within 20 m of the goal or an enemy they lower bayonets into a real charge. They climb out of trenches on the way, do not fire, and ignore suppression and cover reactions, though they still dodge incoming shells. After seven seconds, on reaching the goal, or after landing a blow, each returns to normal squad behaviour. On Ypres, men who reach the enemy trench hold it, as trench-assault survivors do. Bots show *Fixing bayonets*, *Over the top* and *Bayonet charge* as their mode, and the levelled-bayonet pose. The HUD says **OVER THE TOP! · N FOLLOWING**; friendly whistles within 40 m show **OVER THE TOP! · CHARGE!**, and enemy whistles within 60 m **ENEMY WHISTLE · CHARGE INCOMING**. The whistle is a synthesized pea-whistle trill (a short blast, then a long one), followed by a ragged synthesized "hurrah" from the men who go.

The whistle has one 50-second cooldown per team, shown in the ammo panel. Bot squad leaders blow their own: in the last leg of a Ypres assault, from the forward crater into the enemy trench, or when a commanded squad is 8–35 m from an enemy-held flag with the enemy in sight and at least two other riflemen within 30 m. Bots wait 15 seconds after the whistle is ready, so the player usually has first call, and then take about a one-in-three chance every three seconds. A bot whose rifle is empty or still has more than half a second of reload to go, with a visible enemy 3–9 m away on level, open ground, may drop the clip and rush him (a 60% chance, at most once every ten seconds).

**Balance.** By 1915 a charge into alert, entrenched rifles and machine guns mostly died in the open, and so it does here. Chargers have no cover and cannot fire; enemy bots lead a charger's straight run almost fully (90% of the computed lead, against 35–70% for other targets) with 25% less aim error; defenders within 45 m in a charge's path stop being distracted and keep focus on it. A whistle tells enemies within 60 m where it came from. Enemy wire still slows chargers to a walk. A charge that arrives is rewarded: every 0.1 s each defender within 12 m of a charge coming at him adds up a shock score of 0.15 per charger, 0.8 × his suppression, 0.35 if shellfire or a grenade shook him in the last three seconds, 0.35 if the nearest charger comes out of smoke, 0.25 if he is reloading or empty and 0.3 if a comrade within 8 m was just bayoneted, minus 0.3 for a machine gunner and 0.08 per comrade within 6 m (up to three). Crews on an emplaced gun never break. At 0.75 a bot is shaken for about 1.5 s: it crouches, holds fire and backs away, then cannot be shaken again for six seconds. A shaken player gets the suppression effect and a flinch but keeps full control. A lone charge or a distant one never shakes alert, unhurt riflemen; five chargers arriving together, or three with smoke, shells or suppression, usually do.

In a seeded scenario (`src/charge.test.ts`), five riflemen charging an alert machine gunner and two riflemen across 35 m and 45 m of open ground lose seven of ten men and kill one defender. The same two charges out of a smoke screen shake a defender each time, lose two men and kill five of the six defenders. `tools/charge-audit.ts` runs seeded all-bot battles and reports the kill mix and charge outcomes. Three-minute battles on seeds 12345, 777 and 4242, before the whistle and lone charges existed and after:

| Map (3 battles) | Melee share of deaths, before → after | Whistles | Bayonet-charge kills | Charges that landed a blow | Chargers killed while charging |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ypres | 28/597 (4.7%) → 28/603 (4.6%) | 6 | 2 | 1 of 6 | 6 of 19 |
| Village front | 36/226 (15.9%) → 33/212 (15.6%) | 5 | 1 | 1 of 5 | 5 of 40 |
| Lighthouse point | 24/314 (7.6%) → 22/323 (6.8%) | 7 | 6 | 2 of 7 | 8 of 44, plus 8 lone charges |

Before, bots never charged: their close-combat code thrust without sprinting. After, each team whistles about once every three minutes, and the bayonet charge accounts for under 1% of deaths (9 of 1138). Survivors reached the goal in 15 of the 18 whistled charges, but a blow landed in only 4; of 119 levelled-bayonet rushes, 9 struck. The melee share is unchanged within seed noise, so the charge adds moments rather than making the game melee-dominated.

Press **G** to throw a grenade in the direction you're looking. Each soldier gets **three per spawn**. Human throws have a brief **0.2-second wind-up**: a visible hand brings up the grenade, releases it and returns to the rifle. Smoke uses its own visible canister. Inventory is spent and the fuse starts at actual release; death before release cancels the throw. Aiming can still adjust the direction during preparation. Reloading, firing and climbing wait until the throw finishes. The overhand release sits just above eye level so a level throw from the standing firing step clears the lip. Crouched or trench-floor throws still need an upward look to clear solid earth. Release paths sweep the grenade shell through real cover; they cannot start beyond a wall. Human throws follow the look direction with only a 0.1-radian upward bias, then arc under gravity, bounce off terrain/cover, and explode **three seconds after release**, with a flash, expanding blast effect, rising smoke, and sound. The **7 m blast radius** deals up to 140 damage with distance falloff; solid cover blocks damage. Your own grenade can hurt you; teammates and protected spawns take no damage. A level look produces a shallow, visible throw; look upward to lob farther or clear a parapet, or down to drop one nearby. Bot smoke throws retain their existing loft. Throws briefly interrupt rifle fire; a held G does not repeatedly throw. A connected column of thrown-up dirt rises above the trench lip so a burst inside it remains visible. Flash and damage still respect solid earth. Explosive blast smoke is cosmetic. Press **F** for a separate concealing smoke grenade; bots use these during squad advances, while explosive grenade throws remain player-controlled.

## Trench domination

Capture **A and B** in Blue’s front trench and **C and D** in Red’s. All four start neutral. Stand within six meters of a flag in its connected trench space: bank tops and solid walls do not count. A neutral flag takes **15 seconds** to capture; an enemy flag takes 15 seconds to neutralize, then 15 more to claim. Extra soldiers do not speed this up. Both teams present freeze progress and income. Ownership remains after leaving.

World-space objective markers show only a small, dim letter and distance, fading further near the crosshair and while aiming. Contested flags use gold and a small `!`; full names, ownership and capture progress remain in the top HUD, capture readout and tactical map.

Each uncontested flag earns its owner **one point every three seconds**. First to **300** wins; otherwise the higher score after eight minutes wins, with ties drawn. Kills remain personal scoreboard statistics and earn no team points. The HUD shows ownership, contested flags and capture progress; the Q tactical map also marks public flag ownership. Named landmarks help orient the four areas: **A Farmhouse**, **B Shattered Wood**, **C Gun Pit**, and **D Broken Bunker**. Flags remain in their original trench positions; the ruins, shattered trees and abandoned gun stand nearby without closing the existing trench routes.

### Final phase: limited reinforcements

Playtesters found the end of a close match could become an endless trade of charges with nothing at stake. So the last two minutes are a **final phase** on every domination map except Training. It also starts the moment either side reaches **240 points** (80% of the limit). A shortened match (`?duration=`) uses its last quarter, so `?duration=30` starts it with 7.5 seconds left. Before then the HUD notes when it will come.

When it starts, a banner announces it and each side gets a reinforcement pool of **one respawn per soldier**: 50 on the 50v50 maps, 25 on Lighthouse point and 6 in Fort Vaux and the Zeppelin. Each respawn from then on spends one, including a death just before the phase whose five-second wait ends after it. The pools are shown under the score. Your side's count blinks and a banner warns you when it reaches a quarter of the pool (at least 3, and never at the start). Banners also tell you when either side runs dry. At zero, a side's dead stay dead. Flags keep scoring as normal, and the score or the clock still decides the match unless a side with nobody standing has no reinforcements left: it **loses immediately**, whatever the score. If both sides fall on the same tick, the score decides. The results screen gives the reason, for example "Imperial Germany ran out of reinforcements".

When you die with nothing left, the death card reads **No reinforcements** in place of the countdown and says how many of your side still stand. Once the five-second death report has run, the camera follows a living teammate; left-click switches to the next. Redeploy is unavailable from then on.

A side at or below its low mark stops launching over-the-top trench assaults and holds what it has. One respawn per soldier was chosen from seven seeded 8-minute all-bot matches, one per combat map. Bots lose roughly one to three soldiers per man over two minutes, so in five matches the first side ran dry 49 to 90 seconds into the phase, leaving the last minute as a fight that losses decide. That side usually finished outnumbered (12 standing against 44 on Ypres), but no side was wiped out. On Verdun and Village front the score reached 240 early and a side reached 300 with 44 to 68 percent of each pool unspent. Rerun the audit with `tools/reinforcement-audit.ts`; its header explains how to bundle it. Tune the rule in `reinforcementRules` (`src/reinforcements.ts`).

Bots still cover from firing steps and cross in small smoke-screened squads. Assaults target enemy flags; survivors capture them on the floor, then defend nearby steps. Nearby riflemen retake threatened home flags while machine gunners keep covering. Respawns remain behind each team’s rear trench, spread in a line along the whole front rather than in one group (see [Spawning along the front](#spawning-along-the-front)). Industrial retains its original three-sector rules. This is an initial balance prototype.

Each side has a wire belt about five meters in front of its front-line parapet, following the trench's zigzag in 4 m segments. Gaps are left opposite the defenders' two flags and the center. A side's own soldiers pass through its belt freely; the enemy is slowed to a walk and cannot sprint, hop or mantle there. A shell cuts any segment within about 2.4 m and a grenade within 1.4 m; cut segments stay cut and show as broken stakes and a low tangle. The six starting craters hold standing water you splash through.

A barbed-wire belt surrounds the sector, just outside the playable area. Inside the wire you can only walk: no sprinting, hopping or mantling. Once you cross it, a banner counts down five seconds. Stay out and a three-shell salvo walks along the wire onto you, repeating until you return; it credits no one. Stepping back in for a full second clears the warning, but popping in and out does not reset the countdown. Each trench line continues past the north and south wire behind a sandbag block. Bots never leave the playable area.

## Capture the flag

Ypres, Ypres at night and Verdun sector offer a second mode beside domination. Choose **Capture the flag** under the map cards in the briefing, or add `?mode=ctf` to the URL (`?map=trenches&mode=ctf`). Domination stays the default, and other maps ignore the parameter. The mode came from playtest feedback that a domination match on Ypres invites camping one flag: here each side has **three flag pickup points** spread along its own line, so no single spot can be held against every raid, and one **drop point** in its own base.

| | Ypres (by day or at night) | Verdun sector |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blue pickup points | A North bay, B Centre bay, C South bay, on the front trench floor | A Chapitre Wood, B front trench centre, C Fleury |
| Red pickup points | D North bay, E Centre bay, F South bay | D Dame Ravine, E front trench centre, F Hardaumont Work |
| Drop points | The centre bay of each side's support (rear) trench | The centre of each side's rear trench |

Rules and why:

- **Take a flag by touching its stand** (within 2.2 m, at about the same height and with nothing solid between, so not from the bank top above it). It is instant: a raider who reaches a stand alive has earned it. You carry one flag at a time.
- **The carrier walks and fights with the pistol.** Sprint is off, the rifle is holstered and cannot be drawn, frags are held, and emplacements and flame kits cannot be used. Smoke is allowed, to cover the walk home. Mantling and hopping stay, because the trenches cannot be crossed without them. A slow, lightly armed carrier gives defenders time to run him down, so escorts matter.
- **The flag falls where its carrier dies** (or redeploys). A flag that falls beyond the sector wire lands just inside it.
- **Touch your own dropped flag to return it** to its stand at once. Left alone, a dropped flag goes home by itself after **20 seconds**, so a flag can never be lost in a crater. **A teammate can pick up a dropped enemy flag** and carry it on.
- **A capture** happens when a carrier reaches his own drop point (within 3.5 m). It scores **one point**. Your own flags need not be home to score: with three flags a side that rule would only stall matches.
- **A captured flag's pickup point restocks after 30 seconds.** Long enough that raiders move on to another point, short enough that all three stay in play. A returned flag goes straight back to its stand.
- **First to 5 captures wins**, or the higher score after eight minutes. **Tied at full time**, the match goes to sudden-death overtime: the next capture wins. After two more minutes without one it is a draw.

What you see: each pickup point is an ammunition box with a flag on a post, inside a ring painted in its side's colour; the ring turns pale while its flag is away. Drop points are tall signboards in the team colour inside a wider ring. A carrier wears the flag on a short staff across his back, above head height, so he stands out over a parapet. A dropped flag lies on the ground. The top HUD shows each side's three flags (`⚑` carried, a countdown while dropped or restocking) beside **CAPTURE THE FLAG · FIRST TO 5**; screen labels mark stocked stands, every carrier, every dropped flag and your own **DROP** point. The minimap shows the pickup points (hollow when empty), both drop points (◆) and loose flags (⚑). Flag events go to the kill feed and an announcer line ("A. Wright took the flag at E", "Britain captured flag E · 2–1"). Carrying, a gold frame edges the screen and the prompt reads **YOU HAVE FLAG E · TAKE IT TO YOUR DROP POINT** with the distance.

Bots: every half second each team's orders are redrawn from public flag state (which flags are home, dropped where, or on whose back). A carrier heads for his drop point. The four nearest soldiers run down an enemy carrier of their flag, the three nearest go to each dropped flag (to return their own or carry on an enemy's), and up to four within 30 m escort their own carrier, walking a little ahead of him. Three squads in five raid: all parties strike one enemy stand together and move on to the next every 40 seconds, so the defence cannot settle on one. Massed raids replaced parties spread over all three points, which were shot down one by one in the open. The rest man the firing steps (Ypres) or guard their own stands (Verdun). Soldiers with orders to move keep walking while they shoot instead of creeping, leaving their trench they screen the crossing with smoke, and where the route planner has no way across (it can drop into a trench but not climb out) they go straight for the goal and mantle every bank in the way. The scripted trench assaults of domination are off in this mode.

`tools/ctf-audit.ts` plays seeded full matches (bundle it with esbuild for Node; see its header). `src/ctf.test.ts` covers the rules, and a short seeded battle in which both teams capture.

## Ypres at night

Choose **Ypres at night** in the start menu or open `?map=night-raid`. It is Ypres's ground after dark, 160 by 160 m with 50 soldiers a side as by day: the same trenches, wire and four flags, so knowing the day map is knowing this one.

- **You see about fifteen meters.** Beyond that the ground is black. Bots follow the same rule: a soldier in the dark is seen only within 15 m, however clear the line to them.
- **A fire gives you away.** Braziers burn in the rear trenches and light about seven meters around them. A soldier standing in that light is seen from across the field, by players and bots alike.
- **Steer by the lamps.** Lanterns hang at the dugouts and along the trench walls, and each flag has a lamp on its pole. They are shaded: enough to walk toward, too little to be shot by.
- **Follow the tape.** Each side has pegged two lines of white tape through the lanes it left in its own wire.

### Flares

Open the tactical map and press **F** to switch it from a fire mission to a flare, then choose a sector as you would for artillery.

- A flare bursts 42 m up three seconds after the call, sinks on its parachute for 25 seconds, and lights the ground for about 45 m around it. The wind carries it along the front.
- It has its own 20-second cooldown. Calling a flare does not delay a fire mission, and a fire mission does not delay a flare. The HUD shows both.
- Everyone under a flare can be seen from across the field: you, your side, and the enemy.
- Bots send flares over enemies they hear firing in the dark, and now and then over the ground in front of their line.
- At night a bot spotter can only call artillery onto ground it can see, so flares come before barrages.

`src/flares.ts` holds the rules; its light feeds the same `sightLimit`.

### Bursts and muzzle flash

- **A burst shows the ground around it.** For under half a second a shell lights about 36 m and a grenade about 28 m. A barrage strobes no-man's land, and anyone caught in a flash can be glimpsed from across the field.
- **Firing gives you away.** For a second after a shot, the shooter can be seen from up to 60 m.
- **Craters glow.** A fresh crater's embers burn for twenty seconds. They mark where a shell fell; they do not light anyone.
- Smoke and dust are dark at night unless something lights them.

The rule lives in `src/night.ts`: `sightLimit(map, target)` is 15 m in the dark and rises to 90 m beside an open fire. The renderer places a light at every lamp the rule lists, so what you see and what a bot sees agree.

## Zeppelin

Choose **Zeppelin** in the start menu or open `?map=zeppelin`. Six a side fight aboard a German naval airship drifting above a sea of cloud at dusk. French boarders spawn at the stern and the crew at the bow; the ship is the same both ways round.

The **gallery** runs through the gas cells above it: longer sight lines between valve housings. The **top walk** runs along the outside of the hull with nothing but vents for cover.

## Verdun forest trenches

Verdun has irregular woodland belts along both flanks: tall pines and torn broadleaf crowns mixed with bare trunks and snapped trees. Eight denser, asymmetric groves surround Thiaumont and extend into the formerly bare ground on either side. The center has 133 trees, with three broad shooting breaks and clear relay approaches; the whole field has 313 destructible trees, 42 low brush patches, 14 fallen logs and 12 stumps. Trunks provide real cover; foliage affects bot visibility, and the low undergrowth leaves soldier-height sightlines open. Between the front lines four months of shelling have stripped most crowns, leaving bare trunks: only about a quarter keep their foliage in the center and half toward each side's line, while behind the lines the woods stand. Every shell scar between the front lines is dug as a real bowl (the cuts fade out short of the wire), nine of them in open ground where no tree grows, and about twenty hold water that pools below the rim, its edge running under the bank; and the ground between the lines is churned to bare earth even under the trees. Central trees can be stripped and felled further by explosions, opening the field as the match progresses.

The 208 × 128 m field has the same wired edge as Ypres: a barbed-wire belt just outside it slows you to a walk, and staying past it for five seconds draws the boundary salvo. A 16 m dead zone lies between the wire and the edge of the terrain. Bots dodging a barrage only run for cover inside the field.

The woods do not stop at the wire. Where a belt reaches the edge it carries on through the dead zone, and scattered shell-torn trunks stand elsewhere in it; these trees are scenery and cannot be shot or felled. Beyond the terrain the land climbs into wooded hills, shattered near the continuing front line and intact further back. A ruined village with the stump of a church tower lies behind the French side, a fort's earth glacis and concrete front sit on a ridge behind the German side, and observation balloons, smoke columns, telegraph poles and distant shell bursts and flares fill the horizon. Each burst's rumble arrives after its flash, delayed by its distance.

Verdun's overcast sun now stands at 30° rather than 42°, so the trees cast longer, softer shadows, and the haze closes at 260 m. Ground mist drifts through the woods across the whole field, about twice as many banks as Ypres's no-man's land for twice the width, and pools along every other trench section. Smoke rises from the shelter bays and from fresh shell craters, scraps of cloth hang in the boundary wire, and you hear wind in the wire, a machine gun far off and crows over the trees. Low graphics quality leaves the mist, smoke, cloth and moving backdrop out.

Verdun's trenches are dressed like Ypres's, scaled to their 2 m width: sandbags along the parados, framed dugout entrances in the rear walls of the longer rear sections, crates, rifle racks, stretchers, braziers in the shelter bays, periscopes along the front lines, telephone cable and standing water in the sumps. French stretches are revetted mostly with woven hurdles (clayonnage), with some timber and corrugated iron; German stretches keep neat timber and wicker. Painted boards name each trench, in French on the French side, and point along it to the side's nearest flag. All of it is scenery and adds no cover.

Each side has stretches of barbed wire about five meters in front of its front line, in 4 m segments: six on the French side and nine on the German, where the woods leave room. Wherever a sap, communication route, exit ramp or trench comes within two meters, a trunk stands on the wire, or a flag is near, the segment is left out, so every way out of the lines stays open. As on Ypres, a side's own soldiers pass through its wire freely, the enemy is slowed to a walk and cannot sprint, hop or mantle there, and shells and grenades cut it. The flags sit forward of the wire, so it guards the trench lines rather than the objectives; in bot matches it changes nothing. Water stands in the shell scars, the debris of earlier fighting lies across the field between the front lines, and the ground is churned where it was fought over: trodden mud in and beside every trench, earth thrown up around the scars and bare ground across the open field. Under the trees the leaf litter survives.

Three forward sap trenches turn through the woods, joining the existing rear/front network. The southern approaches take different doglegs on each side. Walk-out slopes, objective approaches and worn woodland tracks stay clear of new props. Brown leaf litter, needles and damp soil blend into the same terrain material; fresh artillery and grenade cuts reveal earth. Crowns disappear when trees are badly damaged, and destroyed trunks fall into physical low timber cover using the existing bounded animation/debris system.

The forest presentation uses tapered bark trunks, fine branching limbs, irregular needle sprays and torn broadleaf crowns. Up to 180 small fern, root, branch and curled-leaf details sit on stable ground, add no collision, and disappear where explosions excavate the surface. Shared mesh variants and static batches bound their cost; no foliage animation runs per tree.

The latest forest geometry, floor detail and ambient audio are included in public trial version21. The published JS, CSS and map previews match the tested build byte for byte. See HANDOFF.md for validation and deployment provenance.

Intact leaf-covered ground has crunchy footsteps; duckboards keep their wooden steps and fresh craters sound muddy. Quiet wind/rustling, occasional creaks and off-map distant battle sounds add ambience. Wind rustling weakens as nearby crowns are stripped or felled. Tree damage and falling timber produce positional cracks/thuds, including Verdun's tree IDs. All sounds use the existing volume, pause/reset, capture and voice-budget controls; no spoken callouts were added.

France versus Imperial Germany, 50 soldiers per team, five-flag domination. Choose [Verdun sector](https://sector-control-trial-20261108.cody22597.chatgpt.site/?map=verdun) in the battlefield menu. It is set at Fleury and Thiaumont on 23 June 1916, on an authored forest battlefield rather than a survey of the ground. Its gas shells are phosgene, not mustard, and it has no tanks: none fought at Verdun.

## Spring Offensive

St. Quentin, 21 March 1918, authored for play rather than reconstructed: Britain against Imperial Germany on a 288 × 176 m field (Verdun's is 208 × 128) with the same wired edge and 16 m dead zone. Each side holds three trench lines. Britain's first line is the outpost line, short posts with open ground between them that stormtroopers can slip through; behind it come the battle-zone trench and the rear switch line. Germany has its jump-off trench, front line and support line. The first two lines of each side have firing steps, wire belts about nine metres in front of them and sally ramps toward the enemy; communication trenches join every line to the next, every isolated post has a ramp up its rear wall, and dugout bays open behind the second and third lines. Every line runs on into the dead zone and is closed with sandbags at the boundary, and every line has a filled crossing between z 57 and 67: the tank lane.

**Attack and defend** is the default mode, in two phases: 1, the forward zone, C Hill Redoubt, D Ruined village and E Windmill crossroads; 2, the battle zone, A Battle-zone keep and B Railway cutting. Germany attacks. Britain starts holding every flag but Germany's own F Chalk quarry and G Sunken road, which stay locked in German hands all match. The German assault forms up in front of its jump-off trench and Britain holds a line between its outpost line and the battle-zone trench; once the forward zone falls the Germans come up behind the British outpost line and Britain falls back behind its rear switch line. Germany starts with 6 minutes and 3 reinforcements per soldier, and taking the forward zone adds 4 minutes and 1 more per soldier. Britain redeploys without limit, 15 seconds after falling. Forward deployment is off in this mode, since the spawns move with the front. In 8 seeded 64-a-side bot matches (`tools/offensive-audit.ts`) Germany won 4 and took the forward zone in 7. The rest works as on Gallipoli below. Choose **Domination** under the map cards, or add `?mode=domination`, for the seven-flag domination described next.

Seven-flag domination: A Battle-zone keep (a half-depth earthwork with a concrete machine-gun post) and B Railway cutting (wagons and stores in a shallow cutting) for Britain; C Hill Redoubt (a ring trench round a ruined blockhouse), D Ruined village (eight shelled houses and a roofless church round the square) and E Windmill crossroads (the broken mill on the open plain) in no-man's land; F Chalk quarry (a two-step pit) and G Sunken road (a lane cut into the chalk with ramps up either bank) for Germany. The home flags sit between each side's second and third lines, so taking them means breaking the battle zone.

**Forward deployment** (domination and capture the flag). While your side holds one of its own home flags it opens a deployment point 14 m behind that flag, outside its capture ring, so nobody deploys onto a flag that is being fought over. Soldiers deploying there spread along a 32 m line across the flag's sector, never within 9 m of any flag. While you are down the death card asks you to choose where to deploy and lists the main spawn and every open point, each with its number key drawn as a key cap; press **1** for the rear lines or **2**–**3** for a point. With no point open it says you are deploying at the rear lines. Bots deploy at the open point nearest their objective. Lose the flag and the point closes.

**Morning fog.** The match opens in thick white fog that lifts over the first three minutes, from about 55 m of visibility to 300 m. It is presentation only: bots see as usual. Gas comes only from the gas artillery shell.

**Tanks.** With Tanks ticked the Mark IV and the A7V arrive at tank parks on the open plain to the south and come up the tank lane through the line crossings.

**Players.** 50 a side by default. The map allows up to 64 a side with `?teamSize=64`; the default stays at 50 until frame time at 128 soldiers has been checked on real hardware. Capture the flag puts each side's three pickups on its first line's floor at the north, centre and south posts, with the drop in the battle-zone trench.

## Gallipoli

Anzac, August 1915, authored for play rather than reconstructed: the ANZAC against the Ottoman army on a 208 × 144 m hillside (the terrain runs on to 240 × 176 m) with the sea to the west. Choose **Gallipoli** in the battlefield menu or add `?map=gallipoli`. ANZAC deploys on the beach under the cliffs, by a pier; two warships stand offshore. Cliffs too steep to climb funnel the attack into the scrub gullies and up the spurs. The names are real positions at Anzac; their places on the map are approximate. No gas, tanks, light machine guns, flamethrowers or anti-tank kits: none fought there in 1915, and the light machine-gun slots go to riflemen.

**Attack and defend** is the default mode, in three phases: 1, A Table Top and B Owen's Gully; 2, C The Nek and D Lone Pine; 3, E Scrubby Knoll and F Chunuk Bair. The Ottomans start holding every flag. Only the open phase's flags can be captured; the rest are locked, and taking both flags of a phase opens the next. ANZAC starts with 5 minutes and 2 reinforcements per soldier; each phase taken adds 4 minutes and 1 more per soldier, ANZAC deploys closer behind the line it took, and the Ottomans fall back to the next. The Ottomans redeploy without limit, 10 seconds after falling. ANZAC wins by taking the last phase; the Ottomans win if the clock runs out, or if ANZAC runs out of reinforcements with nobody left standing. The strip under the score shows the phase, ANZAC's reinforcements and each flag as open, taken or locked. Bots attack and defend only the open phase, and don't go to ground inside a locked flag's ring. Choose **Domination** under the map cards, or add `?mode=domination`, for six-flag domination over the same ground. The mode is generic (`MapDefinition.offensive`, `src/offensive.ts`); Gallipoli and Spring Offensive turn it on. A plan can also list `held` flags behind the attacker's own start line, which the attacker keeps, locked, all match.

**The ground.** The Nek is a narrow saddle with a parados behind the Ottoman trench. Lone Pine's Ottoman trenches are roofed with pine logs on posts, and shelling or bombing the posts brings the roof down. Trenches are 2.4 m deep with a firing step.

**Naval gunfire.** ANZAC's artillery is the fleet: the four turrets of the two warships fire the salvos, and the Ottoman battery stands behind the ridge. Ship shells burst with a quarter more blast radius and damage than field artillery (`navalRules.power` in `src/naval-gunfire.ts`), and the death report names them "Naval gunfire". They fall flat, at 8°, so they can't reach dead ground behind a steeper crest: the Ottoman rear behind the last ridge and the gully below the Nek. Those sectors are dimmed on the tactical map, refused when called, and skipped by bots; every flag stays in reach. In 16 seeded 50v50 matches ANZAC won 3 of 8 at field strength and 4 of 8 with the stronger shells, so the map was left as it was.

**Kit.** ANZAC carry the SMLE and throw jam-tin bombs; the Ottomans carry the Mauser 1903 and throw cricket-ball bombs. ANZAC's trench mortar is the Garland, a short barrel on a wooden base; the Ottomans have a light spigot bomb thrower drawn like the Granatenwerfer, marked approximate in the Armory. ANZAC soldiers wear slouch hats and lemon squeezers, the Ottomans the kabalak.

**Periscope rifle.** An ANZAC marksman can deploy with the periscope rifle instead of the scoped rifle: an SMLE in a wooden frame above a box periscope, fired by a wire from a lower grip. Holding aim raises it 0.9 m over about a fifth of a second, enough to fire over a 2.4 m parapet with your head below it. It stops under a roof such as Lone Pine's logs. You aim through the mirror (no magnification), and the aim is loose. Only the mirror shows above the parapet, and bots aim at it: a round through it smashes it without hurting you, and the rifle then aims like any other until your next life. From ANZAC's first trenches the slope still hides the Ottoman line; it pays off from captured Ottoman trenches. Bots don't carry it. Tuning lives in `periscopeRules` (`src/periscope.ts`).

**Players.** 50 a side by default; `?teamSize=64` allows up to 64. The simulation costs about half as much again per tick as Spring Offensive's at 50 a side (`tools/performance-scale.ts gallipoli`), mostly bot route planning through the gullies; the default stays at 50 until frame time at 128 soldiers has been checked on real hardware.

## Tanks

Ypres and Spring Offensive, in either mode, field tanks, and their map cards say so with a **TANKS** badge. Tanks are on by default: untick **Tanks** in the briefing to play without them (the choice is remembered), or add `?tanks=0`; `?tanks=1` turns them back on and `?tank=1` starts the match in command of your side's tank. Each side has one tank at a time: Britain a Mark IV, Germany an A7V. On Ypres they park beside the spawns; on Spring Offensive they start at tank parks on the southern plain and come up the undug tank lane. France's Saint-Chamond is modelled but offered on no map for now: Verdun sector, the only Franco-German trench map, is set in June 1916, and the Saint-Chamond first fought in May 1917. All three share one footprint (8 m by 3.4 m) and the same handling, so neither side is favoured; they differ in where the main gun bears. The Mark IV's 6-pounders sit in side sponsons and fire to either side, the A7V's 57 mm and the Saint-Chamond's 75 mm guns fire forward.

- **Crew.** A bot drives and fights each tank, one crew member for driving and all its guns. When the seat is empty a nearby bot climbs in, or the next one to respawn takes it. Walk up to your own tank and press **E** to take command; its bot climbs out and fights on foot. **E** again climbs out, and a bot takes the tank 15 seconds later. A flag carrier cannot board. The crew are out of reach inside: bullets and bayonets cannot touch them, blasts hurt the tank instead, and they do not count towards holding a flag. Gas still reaches them.
- **Driving.** **W**/**S** throttle, **A**/**D** steer; the camera rides behind and above. Top speed is 1.3 m/s, reverse 0.55 m/s, and the hull turns at about 11° a second. Tanks bridge the maps' ordinary trenches (about 4.5 m across, firing step included) and flatten barbed wire, and climb or crush anything under about 1.25 m, such as sandbags, low walls, crates and logs. Shattered trees go over after a few seconds of pushing. A gap more than 5.5 m wide under both tracks, such as a shell-widened trench or a long junction bay, ditches the tank: it is stuck for 20 seconds while the crew work it free. Now and then the engine stalls for 8 seconds, more often once the tank is damaged. Tanks push soldiers aside and run down enemies they cannot push; they stop for their own side.
- **Guns.** Each tank's machine guns sit in real mounts, each swinging through its own arc: the Mark IV has one in the front plate, one at the back of each sponson and one in the rear door; the A7V two down each side and two in the rear plate; the Saint-Chamond a bow gun, one each side and one astern. Hold the left button and the mount nearest the crosshair that can swing onto it fires, and its tracers leave from that gun (250-round belts, 1,500 rounds in reserve). Hold the right button for the main gun and it fires each time it is loaded and bears, leaving smoke rolling off the barrel: 40 rounds, a 3.5-second reload, a shell that flies flat at 330 m/s (a glowing streak, about a quarter of a second to 90 m) and bursts on whatever it meets with high explosive (the gun sits low in the hull, so if a parapet, sandbags or the lip of a trench beside the tank would stop the shell well short of the crosshair it holds fire, and the panel says the ground is in the way) of about 5 m that breaks cover but barely marks the ground. The panel shows both weapons side by side, each with its ammunition, a loading bar and which gun bears, and warns when the main gun is out of its arc.
- **Damage.** Rifle and machine-gun rounds never damage a tank. Each one that strikes the plates splashes it, more at the vision slits at the front: the commander's view blurs and the guns' aim spreads, and a bot driver slows. Hull damage comes only from blasts against or near the plates, falling off fast with distance: about six grenades on the roof or against the side, two or three close shells, or several tank-gun hits. Below 35% the tank throws a track and is immobilized, but its guns keep fighting. At zero it is knocked out: the crew bail out with burns and the wreck stays where it stopped, blocking movement and bullets. Each side keeps its last two wrecks. A replacement arrives at the tank park 90 seconds later.
- **Bots against tanks.** Bots shoot at a tank's crew, which splashes it, throw grenades at it as at any target, and commanders without a human on their side call artillery on an enemy tank within reach before anything else.

## Hip fire

A round fired from the shoulder goes where the sights point, apart from recoil. Fired from the hip, or before the sights are fully up, it lands anywhere inside a cone that narrows as the weapon comes up to the eye (`src/hip-fire.ts`). The cone's half-angle is 3.4° for a bolt-action rifle, 2.6° for a carbine, 4.3° for a scoped rifle, 1.7° for a pistol and 2.9° for a light machine gun: a hip shot from a rifle lands within about 1.8 m of the aim point at 30 m. Moving faster than a slow walk widens it by half, and crouching narrows it a fifth. A light machine gun also walks off target while its gunner moves, raised or not. Bots fire through the same rule: they wait for their sights to come up before squeezing the trigger, so an unmasked bot always shoots aimed, and a masked bot, which cannot aim, shoots from the hip. A masked bot also scatters each shot 3.5 times as widely as it fires (misted glass and narrow eyepieces, as the player sees them), so the mask costs it accuracy at point-blank range too, where the hip cone alone is small: in seeded matches masked bots hit about half as often as aimed ones at every range, 23% against 46% under 8 m.

## Gas shells

Gas shells are an alternate artillery strike on every map that offers artillery. They carry mustard gas on the 1917 maps and phosgene on Verdun sector, which is set in June 1916, a year before mustard was first used; the rules are the same, but on Verdun the tactical map and notices say PHOSGENE SHELLS, deaths read "Phosgene shell", the masked notice says MASK LEAKING and the cloud is a paler grey-white (`gasAgent` in `src/gas.ts`). The rules live in `src/gas.ts`; the HUD, map clouds, lens and sounds in `src/gas-ui.ts`; the clouds as drawn in `src/gas-view.ts`.

- **Calling gas.** Press **F** on the tactical map to switch the fire mission from high explosive to gas shells (on night maps F steps through artillery, flare and gas), then pick a sector. Gas follows the same rules as a fire mission: range, a human caller, one mission at a time and the same 30-second artillery cooldown, so each call is gas *or* high explosive. Gas shells cannot land within 24 m of either team's deployment.
- **The shells.** Four gas shells whistle in like a high-explosive salvo and land within about 4 m of the aim point over the best part of a second. Each lands with a dull plop rather than a blast: a small bursting charge throws up a spit of earth and a dense jet of yellow-green gas that hangs for a few seconds. They dig no crater and hurt nobody on impact.
- **The cloud.** Three seconds after the call, as the first shell lands, a low cloud spreads to about 18 m across and lingers 25 seconds, drifting along the front at 0.3 m/s. It burns anyone unmasked, up to 12 HP a second at its heart, your own side included. Kills on the enemy are credited to the caller; deaths read "Gas shell". Clouds show on the minimap and tactical map.
- **Gas mask.** Every soldier carries one. Press **M** (remappable) to put it on: it takes a second with both hands, so you cannot fire, throw, aim, climb or finish a reload meanwhile. Taking it off takes half a second and protection ends at once. You cannot aim down sights while the mask is on: the eyepieces sit where the sights would, so every shot is fired from the hip (see Hip fire below), and bots follow the same rule. Masked, gas burns 1.5 HP a second, because mustard still blisters skin through a mask. In first person you see out through two round glass eyepieces in pressed metal rims, set apart by the bridge of the nose: the facepiece comes up from under the chin as it goes on, the glass mists over as it seals and then a little with each breath out, and you hear yourself breathe; other soldiers show the facepiece, eyepieces and filter tin. A masked bot notices enemies only within about 40° of where it looks, against 63° bare-faced. You redeploy bare-faced.
- **Bots** mask up within 8 m of a cloud, walk out of it, route around it and take the mask off once 18 m clear for five seconds. A bot team's caller chooses gas over high explosive for a knot of three or more enemies 30–70 m away and clear of its own men.
- **Look.** Each cloud is a ring of yellow-green billboards sitting on the ground. Quads within 6 m of the camera are left out, as for mist, so standing in one never washes out the view; a yellow haze at the screen edges says you are in it instead, halved through a mask.

## Soldiers

Soldiers have rounded, tapered limbs and tailored coats, smaller human-shaped heads, curved steel helmets, puttees and oval boots. Muted team-colored uniforms, armbands, webbing, pouches, packs and rolled blankets retain team readability. The models are stylized procedural figures. Stride timing, upper-body sway and idle breathing vary between soldiers. Reloads visibly load a service-rifle clip or exchange the other weapons’ magazines; the right hand works the action while the left stays on its grip. Walking, crouching, mantling, blast reactions and falling bodies retain their articulated poses.

A killed soldier becomes a physical ragdoll immediately: 13 articulated body parts and a loosely held weapon, joined with limited hips, shoulders, elbows, knees, neck and ankles. Bodies inherit movement and react to hit/blast impulses; earth, trench walls and nearby cover stop them. Nearby bodies remain for about 20 seconds, fading at the end; at most 24 are retained. They settle, can be moved by later explosions, and fall again when supporting earth or cover is destroyed. They are cosmetic for gameplay, so they do not block movement, shots or objectives. Physics uses fixed 60 Hz steps and automatic sleeping; one skinned mesh per body keeps rendering bounded. Respawning soldiers are independent of the retained body. Pausing freezes the presentation and a fresh match clears it.

Spoken soldier callouts are removed, including smoke, grenade and incoming-artillery lines. Weapon fire, explosions, incoming-shell whistles, footsteps and other battlefield effects remain audible.

## Battlefield sound and reactions

Rifles, explosions and incoming shells now have directional sound, distance filtering and cover muffling. Nearby rounds produce sharp spatial cracks using the actual unobstructed near-miss events. Rifle tails soften with distance; artillery has a falling whistle, a sudden broadband crack and an unpitched low rumble. Explosions can take a voice from quieter rifle chatter so their sound is not lost in a crowded mix. Sounds are synthesized locally as a prototype.

**Every shell has its own voice on the way in**, so you can tell by ear what is coming and where (src/shell-sound.ts). A field howitzer's shell whistles down over a tearing rush for about a second. The Ypres stray heavy rushes in for two and a half seconds with a deep roar, like a train coming on. A warship's flat shell at Gallipoli is a whizz-bang: a quarter-second rip, then the burst. Gas shells wobble as they tumble. Each army's trench mortar sounds different: the Stokes bomb's two-note wail, the Granatenwerfer's fast whirr, the crapouillot's heavy wobble, the Garland jam-tin's end-over-end whoosh and the Ottoman spigot bomb's lower whirr. Each voice moves along the shell's real path, from the firing side down to where it lands, and is heard only when that path comes close enough. Now and then a heavy shell passes high over the sector toward the rear areas and lands far off a few seconds later. It is never aimed at the sector. All of this is sound only: shells, timing and damage are unchanged.

Footsteps follow distance actually walked: mud outside the trenches, wood on visible duckboards, and concrete in the industrial district. Sprinting is louder and crouching quieter. Planks disappear where excavation removes their support, and footsteps there return to mud. Nearby soldiers contribute footsteps; airborne or stationary soldiers do not.

Nearby explosions briefly jolt the camera, muffle battlefield sound and add falling-dirt audio. Cover reduces the reaction, hearing recovers over a few seconds, and nearby soldier rigs briefly brace. These are presentation effects and do not alter input, aim, damage or bot decisions. Start/pause menus include a **Battlefield volume** slider (zero mutes) and a **Blast camera motion** toggle. Headphones help with direction.

Recorded playtests now include the game's stereo output, along with presentation counters and a bounded recent-sound log in the JSON. They capture no microphone or desktop audio. Sound voices and audible footsteps are capped for the 100-soldier battle; pausing cancels active/scheduled sounds and restarting clears feedback.

## Destruction prototype

Grenades now excavate persistent craters and damage wooden obstacles, trench lining, and sandbags in both maps. A ground-level grenade can dig roughly 0.5 m at its center across an initial 3.5 m footprint, with a smooth rounded bowl; airbursts dig less. Repeated hits deepen and widen the bowl, with a lowest floor of −0.9 m. Craters affect walking height, cover, bullets, camera collision, and bot routes. Parapet crests can be chipped, and repeated concentrated hits can create a breach. Wooden lining can break away while the earth behind it remains solid. When excavation removes its backing, the remaining lining breaks away too, so a walkable breach cannot retain a standing wooden facade. Sandbags lose height before disappearing. Two light wooden screens behind the trench lines provide additional destructible cover.

Explosions against an exposed trench face now cut rounded chunks sideways into the bank. Repeated grenade hits or a strong artillery strike can make an **open, walkable breach** with matching terrain collision; partial cuts leave earth that still stops shots. Intact solid screens block excavation.

Artillery bowls now vary in size, depth, elliptical orientation, offset and softly irregular rims, deterministically per impact. Low firing-step ends crumble locally into rounded pockets when directly struck; relaxation cannot carve remote intact trench edges. Meshes, rays and movement share the same deformed surface. Artillery also slumps damaged narrow earth remnants into low rubble slopes. Partially eroded bank edges blend consistently between terrain samples, preventing thin upright sheets with collision. Intact distant walls and fixed foundations remain protected.

Scattered grass, shrubs and battle-scarred trees break up the rear banks and flanks while no man’s land stays relatively open. Most trees are bare or snapped, with exposed roots and splintered tops; a few retain small, muted crowns. Bare branches provide no invisible foliage concealment, while trunks remain solid and destructible. Weathered, split timber posts and low abandoned helmets, packs and planks dress the trench edges; this small clutter is cosmetic and clears where explosions excavate its footing. Foliage obscures bot sight but lets soldiers and bullets pass; trunks are solid. Explosions clear brush, strip tree crowns and fell trunks into destructible logs and stumps that become new cover. Rifle impacts can chip and eventually fell trees too. Roots exposed by excavation also fail. Squads can choose newly excavated craters as regrouping cover and crouch in deeper bowls. Flying dirt and fragments remain cosmetic. Trees now tip over with an accelerating fall, a small landing bounce and a timed timber thud before settling into their log and stump. Wood splinters, leaves and dirt tumble, bounce against the terrain and settle briefly before fading. These effects pause with the game and remain cosmetic.

The trench landmarks add sectional destruction: brick farmhouse walls, a concrete bunker and an abandoned gun can take blast damage. Damaged wall sections lose height; destroyed sections disappear and leave low rubble that blocks movement and shots until further blasts clear it. The farmhouse roof patch and bunker slab collapse when too few tall supports remain. Excavated footing can also bring down unsupported sections. Dust, chunky fragments and collapse sounds accompany the change; flying fragments are cosmetic, while settled rubble is real cover. Restart restores everything.

Industrial buildings, bunkers, containers and concrete barriers retain their existing fixed geometry. Terrain remains a height field without enclosed tunnels; landmark roofs are separate supported box geometry. This is a sectional-collapse prototype rather than a general structural physics system. Explosion profiles keep soldier damage, structural damage and excavation settings separate.

Destruction events record the source, world revision, object health/stage, changed terrain samples and rebuilt patches. JSON snapshots include the accumulated world state. The renderer replaces only affected local terrain meshes/colliders and refreshes damaged cover; bots recalculate routes after changes.

## Artillery prototype

Press **Q** to open a quick tactical map, then click a broad **16 m sector** to request fire. Press Q again or right-click to cancel. The battle keeps moving; movement and crouch still work, while aiming/firing/throws are suppressed. The map shows the authored trench layout and your position, without live enemy markers. Mouse capture stays active during normal play, using a visible map cursor.

You can request artillery from the trench floor or behind cover. Calls are approximate: the salvo center may drift up to **6 m with a clear view**, or **10 m when blind**, followed by the existing **14 m shell scatter**. You cannot refine a call into a precise point. Range remains **15–70 m**, evaluated to the sector center; unavailable sectors are dimmed with a reason. Charge/cooldown and human-team ownership still apply. Map-edge impacts stay inside the battlefield.

Each team has one shared charge: first available **10 seconds** into the match, then **30 seconds** after each confirmed call. Death does not replenish it or cancel an ordered barrage. If a team has a human player, only that human can call its artillery, including while waiting to respawn. Teams made entirely of bots coordinate their own calls. In normal play this reserves Blue's charge for you; autoplay has no human-controlled soldiers, so both teams can call automatically.

An ordered barrage takes roughly **four seconds** to reach the battlefield, without an enemy countdown or ground warning ring. Listen for the actual incoming shells. Eight shells scatter within **14 m** of the target and land across the same **four-second first-to-last window**, with irregular gaps of roughly **0.23–0.93 seconds**. Visible shells descend in **0.8 seconds**, whistle, and burst on the first solid surface, including bunker roofs.

Each shell deals **240 maximum damage with a 9 m falloff radius**: an unshielded soldier can be killed within roughly five meters. Intact earth fully shields blocked bodies, and partial cover scales artillery damage by how much of the body is exposed. Moving off the firing step or crouching behind the lip offers strong protection; a direct hit inside the trench remains deadly. Craters are smaller: nominal **1.9 m radius / 1 m depth**, terrain reach 3.5 m and bank-cut depth 1.2 m. Structures take damage within 5.25 m of a burst. Irregular shape, dust, flying dirt, structural damage and repeated excavation remain. Fixed buildings/bunkers protect their foundations. Self damage applies; teammates and protected spawns retain grenade protection. Artillery dust is cosmetic; dedicated smoke grenades conceal vision.

Bots react to nearby physical shell flight and impacts, then seek earth cover or the screened rear shelter bays; they no longer know about calls before shells arrive. Survivors return to nearby intact stations. **Future request: creeping barrage**, a fire line that advances slowly forward; deliberately deferred.

Typed `artilleryCall`, `artilleryLaunch`, `artilleryImpact`, `artilleryEvade` and `artilleryEnd` events link mission/shell IDs; snapshots include team cooldowns, missions, shells and retreat state. Recorder summaries count calls, impacts and retreats. Runtime terrain cuts remain sparse/serializable with a revision-based numeric lookup cache for movement and rays.

## Suppression, smoke and squad prototype

Press **F** to throw your **one smoke grenade per spawn**, independent of your three G explosives. The canister uses the same swept/bouncing physics, starts releasing smoke after roughly 2.5 seconds once it lands, and creates a growing **6.5 m radius cloud lasting 20 seconds**, including buildup/fade. Smoke causes no damage or excavation. It blocks vision for both bot teams and artillery spotting, while bullets still pass through it. Bots immediately drop obscured targets and must reacquire/react after visibility returns. Close silhouettes can still be seen. Explosive-grenade/artillery dust is a separate cosmetic effect.

Bots hold their canisters for the first ten seconds of each life. Away from planned trench assaults, selected riflemen screen an exposed crossing when they have taken incoming fire or recently spotted an enemy ahead. They save their canister if there is already smoke or another canister nearby.

Enemy rounds passing within 1.8 m build suppression only along their unobstructed path. Solid cover, teammates and spawn protection are respected; a round can suppress each soldier once. Suppression recovers when incoming fire stops. Suppressed bots lose accuracy and may leave their firing steps for about 1.5 seconds before returning. Human players hear nearby cracks and get a subtle edge effect; their aim/movement stay under direct control. There is no persistent suppression status line.

In trenches, **groups of three or four bots** periodically screen an advance while most soldiers maintain covering fire. Groups wait for a smoke screen, mantle out, sprint to a crater, regroup, lay another screen, and try to reach the opposite front trench. Surviving soldiers who enter the opposing trench hold their forward position until death. Humans are never assigned automatic movement. Nearby physical shells and impacts can interrupt an advance so bots seek earth cover. Waves require enough healthy, armed soldiers at a suitable firing step; starts and outcomes vary with combat. A new launch window opens every 12–18 seconds when enough soldiers are ready. Up to two squads can overlap on different launch areas, with at most eight bots committed, holding forward positions, or still returning from the field. Nearby craters and existing smoke influence route choice. A surviving member takes over if the smoke carrier dies or is reloading. Soldiers wait for thick smoke before the first departure; once committed to a leg they finish the dash even if its screen fades. Survivors already in the field who have exhausted their smoke regroup for three more seconds, then risk an unscreened dash to the next cover. Failed groups do not receive free invulnerability or replacement smoke. Industrial retains its sector behavior.

Trench flags now score through domination. Smoke and covering fire create opportunities to cross and contest enemy flags. Forward respawns, healing and player squad commands remain future work.

Snapshots include smoke, suppression, duck state and squad plans/progress. Typed `smokeThrow`, `smokeBloom`, `smokeEnd`, `nearMiss`, `suppression`, `duck`, `assaultStart`, `assaultProgress` and `assaultEnd` events support log-only checks. Recorder summaries count smoke, pinned bots, assaults and crater/trench arrivals. Tests may set `assaults: false` to isolate the original stationary trench geometry; normal trench play enables assaults.

## Ypres prototype

Choose **Ypres** on the start screen, or open [the 50v50 trench map](http://localhost:5173/?map=trenches). Blue has you plus 49 bots; Red has 50 bots. Industrial remains the default 5v5 map.

The map was called Trench front until it was given a setting: Frezenberg, east of Ypres, on 16 August 1917, when the 16th (Irish) and 36th (Ulster) Divisions attacked across the flooded Hanebeek valley toward German pillboxes in ruined farms. The British flags are **Hanebeek Farm** (A) and **Frezenberg Copse** (B), both invented in the style of British trench maps; the German flags take the names of real strongpoints from that attack, **Beck House** (C) and **Borry Farm** (D). The roofless Cloth Hall and St Martin's tower of Ypres stand on the western skyline behind the British line.

The 160 × 160-meter battlefield (128 m along the line before it was widened; each half of the line moved 10 m out from the centre crossing, so no-man's land is still about 45 m across) has **four zigzag trench lines: two per team**. Each team enters its rear line from open-ground spawns, then follows short dogleg communication trenches into its front line. Blue has longer uneven stretches and setbacks; Red has more frequent, independently placed bends. Six alternate connections create short loops around earth islands, and six recessed 4 × 4 m shelter bays have screened, turning access from the rear lines. These are open-topped earth shelters: a direct shell can still kill. The six original communication routes bridge **16–20 m** between paired lines, with offset entrances and earth traverses blocking straight shots. Spawns remain behind the rear lines. **The front parapets are continuous: there are no ramps or walk-through exits into no man's land.** Rear entry ramps remain.

Support and communication trenches retain **2 m floor lanes**, with compact dogleg turns instead of broad junctions. Rounded bank shoulders open outward by roughly 0.65–0.8 m, softening the corners while keeping the floor narrow. Front firing trenches are **4 m across**, including a two-meter floor lane, a one-meter ramp and a one-meter firing step. The six rear shelter bays are smaller, and rear entry ramps are four meters across. Timber, duckboards, abandoned kit and bot routes follow the tighter layout.

The battlefield starts with **rolling ground and broad hollows**, across no man's land and the rear areas, plus six real shell craters and five shallow depressions. The broad landforms dip by up to about 0.95 m; rear rises reach roughly 0.3 m, while central crests stay low enough to preserve firing-step sightlines. Six larger 0.55–0.7 m shell bowls have rotated, uneven outlines, with nine smaller 0.18–0.24 m hollows overlapping their shoulders and the broad depressions. Scattered clods vary in size and angle, replacing identical blocks around circular rims. The six major bowls remain the bots’ starting cover anchors. Slopes blend back to level around trench lips, floors, steps, rear entry ramps, spawns and fixed cover. Movement, bullets and camera collision share the visible surface; foliage roots follow it. Grenades and artillery continue excavating this starting terrain, and restarting restores it.

Fifty distinct firing positions per side are spread along longer steps, with 1.2 m spacing. The short Blue setback keeps four positions. Spawn formations start five meters farther back to keep the larger roster off the entrance ramps.

Trenches are **2.4 m deep**, with **0.85 m firing steps** along the front edges. The floor conceals a standing soldier from the opposing trench. On a step, the head/helmet and raised rifle clear the parapet while the torso stays behind earth. Walk back off the step to shelter. Cover and visibility come from the actual world geometry; shots aimed below the parapet strike the bank.

Most bots hold individual firing positions inside their own front trench and aim at exposed heads. Small groups now use smoke to advance through crater cover and into the opposing front trench. Bots first notice an enemy in their view, build recognition and turn their rifle toward them; close movement or recent nearby gunfire can draw their attention outside that view. Recognition takes roughly 0.9–1.5 seconds, with additional time to turn. They aim from periodically sampled last-seen positions and imperfect movement estimates. Aim wanders, individual shots vary, and movement/suppression make it worse. They fire uneven groups of two or three semi-auto shots with pauses, and lose focus after 2–4 seconds for a 0.8–1.8 second reassessment. They stop firing when sight is lost and briefly search the last known position before reacquiring. Each soldier has different timings. Players use the same recoil system; bot aim drift remains separate. Trench scoring uses the four domination flags described above; kills remain individual statistics. Industrial retains A/B/C territory scoring. No man's land remains sparsely covered with two short sandbag remnants, wire patches, and shell scars.

Walk over a downward edge to fall naturally. Walk near an upward edge and press **Space** to mantle out. The prompt appears when facing a nearby ledge; movement direction takes priority while walking. A climb takes 1.2 seconds: approach, reach with the free right hand, pull up, cross with tucked knees, and stand on the ledge. The left hand stays on the tucked rifle. Foot/torso/head clearance and a stable landing are checked; firing/capturing pauses during the climb. When there is no valid mantle, Space gives a small hop (about 0.45 m high, half a second airborne), with a knee tuck and soft landing. Upward motion stops at ceilings; airborne presses do not trigger another hop. Hops retain normal horizontal collision, so they do not bypass banks or solid cover. Mantling climbs terrain banks rather than buildings or solid cover. Manual mantling still lets you deliberately go over the top, fully exposed; assault squads also mantle terrain edges through the same movement checks. The prototype retains the current assault rifle and visible traveling rounds; historical weapons are a separate design pass.

## Rules and architecture

All simulation uses 30 Hz ticks, plain serializable state, shared human/bot commands, typed events, and an injected collision boundary. `simulation.ts` runs in Node without PlayCanvas or a DOM. `map.ts` is the shared geometry and objective definition. `rendering.ts` adapts PlayCanvas/Ammo and procedural models. `main.ts` owns browser input, menus, audio, and the frame accumulator.

Industrial starts on flat ground with axis-aligned obstacles. Both maps support runtime grenade excavation. The trench map adds a shared height field, recessed floors, firing steps, and inclined ramps. Movement uses a conservative swept footprint with short substeps and wall sliding; static PlayCanvas/Ammo colliders handle line of sight, bullets, and camera queries. Soldier hit volumes are simulation-owned spheres approximating a capsule. Rendering never determines damage, reload, or capture timing. A future server needs its own trusted collision implementation, command validation, and networking.

Capture requires ten seconds from neutral to ownership; enemy sectors require ten seconds of neutralization followed by ten seconds of capture. Empty progress persists, both teams pause it, and defenders reverse neutralization. There is no capture speed bonus for extra soldiers. Dead soldiers cannot capture. Respawn protection lasts two seconds, permits capturing, and ends upon firing. Rifle rounds visibly travel at 128 m/s, expire after 60 meters, and deal damage on impact. Teammates block rounds without taking damage; protection is checked when the round arrives. Reserve ammo is finite until respawn.

## Local release scope

Primitive art, articulated block soldiers with bending knees/elbows, walking and sprinting motion, synthesized audio, two selectable maps, one rifle and throwable grenades. No backend, runtime CDN assets, deployment, accounts, progression, minimap, or kill feed. Hosting is deferred. Dependencies are locked; the Ammo package includes its own embedded runtime.

Use a WebGL 2 desktop browser. Manual release verification should include Chrome and Firefox, an eight-minute match, restarts, pointer-lock loss, movement against every obstacle, close-cover shooting, and 1080p frame/memory profiling on the target machine. Passing unit tests does not establish a GPU performance guarantee.
